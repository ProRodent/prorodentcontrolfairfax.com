How to Get Rid of Mice in Your House in Fairfax, VA

Getting rid of mice for good takes more than a few traps from the hardware store, and understanding why helps you decide what to try yourself and when to call for help.

Start by confirming it is actually mice.

Before doing anything else, confirm the animal. Mouse droppings are small, about 1/8 to 1/4 inch, and pointed at the ends. Rat droppings run larger, closer to 1/2 to 3/4 inch, and are blunt. Mice also leave smaller, more scattered rub marks along baseboards, while rats tend to leave bigger gnaw marks and outdoor burrows. Getting this right matters, since the fix for each animal differs in scale and approach.

In Fairfax, house mice are the most common indoor rodent, though deer mice sometimes show up in garages, sheds and crawl spaces near wooded or brush-covered lots.

Why a few traps from the store often fail.

Indoors, mice breed all year. A female can have 5 to 10 litters annually, with 5 or 6 pups each time, and gestation runs about three weeks. That means a pair heard in early fall can be a sizable colony by winter's end if nothing closes off their food or entry points. A few snap traps catch the mice that wander into them, but the unsealed gap that let the first mouse in keeps letting new ones through.

Where to place traps for the best chance.

Mice travel along edges, not open floor space. Place traps against walls, behind appliances, in the back of cabinets and near any visible droppings or gnaw marks. In a Fairfax home, that often means checking along the garage wall where it meets the slab, near the crawl space access door, and along attic joists if you suspect activity overhead.

Peanut butter and dried fruit work better as bait than cheese for most mice, since mice are drawn to high-calorie, easy-to-carry food. Check traps daily and reset promptly.

Finding and sealing the entry point.

A mouse needs only about 1/4 inch, roughly the width of a pencil, to squeeze through a gap. Common spots in Fairfax homes include torn crawl space vent screens, garage door thresholds with visible daylight underneath, and gaps where utility lines pass through exterior walls. Older homes near Old Town often show settled gaps at chimney flashing and window sills that developed over decades.

Steel wool and spray foam are common do-it-yourself choices, but both have limits. Steel wool rusts and loosens over time, and foam gets chewed through within a season, especially through a freeze-thaw winter. Hardware cloth, sheet metal and mortar hold up far longer. Our rodent exclusion service uses these longer-lasting materials matched to the size and location of each gap.

When trapping alone is not enough.

If you have set several traps for more than a week with no clear progress, or if you are hearing activity in multiple rooms or an attic you cannot safely access, it is a sign the problem is bigger than a quick fix. Our mouse control service starts with an inspection to find every entry point, not just the one you happened to notice.

An inspection and estimate visit is also worth booking if you are not fully sure whether you are dealing with mice, rats, or both, since the droppings and gnaw evidence can look similar at a glance.

Cleanup after the mice are gone.

Once you are confident the mice are gone, do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings, since that can stir up particles carrying hantavirus risk, according to CDC guidance. Ventilate the area for at least 30 minutes, wear gloves, and wet the droppings with disinfectant before wiping them up. Bag contaminated material and wash your hands thoroughly afterward.

Preventing a repeat next fall.

Cooler fall weather in Northern Virginia is when most homeowners notice the first signs of the season, as mice push toward shelter before the coldest months. Sealing entry points before that seasonal push begins gives you the best chance of avoiding a repeat, rather than reacting once mice are already inside.

What kitchens and food storage need beyond trapping.

Mice go where food is easy to reach, so trapping without also addressing food access tends to slow progress. Store dry goods, cereal and pet food in hard, sealed containers rather than the original cardboard or thin plastic packaging, since mice chew through both easily. Wipe down counters and sweep up crumbs each evening rather than letting them sit overnight, and take out kitchen trash regularly rather than letting it accumulate, since an overflowing bin is as attractive to a mouse as an open food container.

Garages, attics and yards need a different plan than a kitchen.

A mouse problem centered in a garage or attic responds to a different set of fixes than one centered in a kitchen. In a garage, switching from cardboard boxes to sealed plastic bins removes both nesting material and shelter in one step. In an attic, check for nesting material along joists near the roofline and any spot where trees overhang close enough to give mice a climbing route. Outdoors, keep woodpiles raised off the ground and away from the house, and trim brush and dense landscaping back from the foundation, since these features give mice cover during the approach to your home.

When one part of the house clears up but another does not.

It is common for trapping to succeed in one area, say a kitchen, while activity continues in a garage or attic, since these are often separate entry points rather than one connected problem. Treat each area on its own terms rather than assuming success in one part of the house means the whole property is clear. Keep checking areas where you have not yet seen direct evidence, since mice in one part of a house often mean entry points exist elsewhere too.

Quick checklist for a mouse problem.

  • Confirm it is mice, not rats, by checking droppings size and shape.
  • Place traps along walls and near suspected entry points, not in open floor space.
  • Store food, including pet food, in sealed hard containers.
  • Do not sweep dry droppings; ventilate and wet them with disinfectant first.
  • Seal any gap of 1/4 inch or more once the active mice are cleared.

Common questions

How long does it take to get rid of mice completely?

A small problem can settle within a week or two of consistent trapping and sealing. An established colony with multiple entry points can take several weeks and a few follow-up visits.

Should I use poison or traps?

Snap traps and tamper-resistant stations are generally safer around pets and children than loose poison, which can lead to a hidden die-off inside a wall and puts non-target animals at risk.

Can mice come back after I seal my house?

New wear, such as a torn vent screen years later, can eventually open a fresh gap, but a properly sealed home significantly reduces the chance of a quick repeat.

Is it normal to hear mice more at night?

Yes, mice are most active after dark, so scratching, scurrying or squeaking sounds at night are a common and expected sign of activity.

Do I need a professional, or can I handle it myself?

A small, recently noticed problem is sometimes manageable with careful trapping and sealing. A larger or ongoing problem, or one you cannot fully access to inspect, usually benefits from a professional visit.
