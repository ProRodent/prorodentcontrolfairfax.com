Where traps actually go.

A trap set in the middle of a room rarely catches anything, because rodents travel along edges: walls, baseboards, pipes and beams. We place traps and stations along the rub marks, droppings and gnaw evidence found during inspection, in spots such as behind appliances, along crawl space joists and near the garage threshold.

For rats, that often means along a fence line, near a shed foundation or beside a burrow entrance rather than inside the house at all, since Norway rats around Fairfax spend much of their time outdoors near Accotink Creek and Daniels Run.

Snap traps, stations and what we avoid.

We use snap traps and tamper-resistant bait stations, placed out of reach of children, pets and wildlife. We avoid loose bait scattered in the open, since it puts pets at risk and can leave a poisoned rodent to die inside a wall, where the smell lingers for weeks. Glue boards are avoided too, since they catch non-target animals and often leave a rodent alive and suffering for hours.

Checking and clearing on a schedule.

Traps and stations are checked on a schedule that matches how active the problem is. A heavy infestation gets more frequent checks early on, tapering off as catches slow down. We remove trapped animals promptly and reset as needed, rather than leaving a trap unchecked for weeks.

For rat burrows near a creek bank or fence line, we also watch for renewed digging between visits, since a burrow can go quiet for a stretch and then become active again once weather or food sources shift.

Trapping alone is not the finish line.

Removing the rodents you can catch does not close the gap they came through. New animals follow the same scent trail in, and a house that only gets trapping tends to need repeat visits indefinitely. That is why trapping pairs with rodent exclusion once the active population is under control.

  • We identify travel routes from droppings, rub marks and gnaw evidence before placing anything.
  • We choose snap traps or tamper-resistant stations based on the site and who else uses the space.
  • We check and clear on a schedule tied to how active the problem is.
  • We recommend sealing once trapping brings the active population down, so new rodents cannot restart the cycle.

Attics, crawl spaces and yards need different setups.

An attic trap plan differs from a crawl space plan, and both differ from a yard plan for a rat burrow. Attics need traps placed along joists near entry points, not scattered across open insulation. Crawl spaces need traps near the access door and along the foundation wall. Yards need stations near burrow entrances and along fence lines, out of reach of pets that use the same yard.

If nesting material is already visible in an attic or crawl space, our attic and crawl space rodent cleanup service addresses that mess once the active animals are cleared.

What to expect during a trapping visit.

The first visit sets traps and stations based on the inspection findings. Follow-up visits check catches, note where activity has slowed and where it has not, and adjust placement if needed. We explain what we found at each check so you know how the job is progressing.

Before we arrive.

  • Keep pets and children away from any known burrow or nest area.
  • Do not move or disturb traps you may have set yourself.
  • Do not add loose bait or poison on your own before we assess the site.
  • Note any new activity, droppings or noises since your last call.
  • Clear access to the crawl space, attic hatch or fence line where possible.

When the work is considered done.

We consider trapping complete once catches stop and no fresh droppings, gnaw marks or burrow activity appear across a few checks in a row. At that point we recommend sealing any remaining gaps found during the rodent inspection so the same route does not reopen next season.

Larger infestations and multiple visits.

A property with an established infestation, rather than a single mouse that wandered in, typically needs more than one or two visits before catches taper off. We would rather set an honest expectation upfront than promise a one-visit fix that a heavier infestation is unlikely to deliver. We explain at each check how the numbers are trending so you can see the progress rather than just taking our word for it.

Working around pets during trapping.

Households with dogs or cats need placements chosen with extra care, since a curious pet investigating a trap poses a different risk than one placed in an area pets cannot reach. We ask about pets during the initial call and again on site, and we adjust placement locations specifically around where your pets actually spend time rather than a generic assumption about where pets usually are in a typical home.

Questions about rodent trapping and removal

How many traps will you set?

It depends on the size of the property and how many entry points and travel routes the inspection finds. We would rather place fewer traps in the right spots than many in the wrong ones.

Do you use poison?

We use tamper-resistant bait stations in some cases, placed out of reach of children and pets. We avoid loose bait because of the risk to pets, wildlife and the chance of a hidden die-off inside a wall.

How often will you check the traps?

Checks are scheduled based on how active the problem is, more frequent early on and less often as catches slow down.

Can I set my own traps between visits?

You can, but tell us so we can account for it during our checks and avoid resetting a trap you have already placed.

Is trapping enough on its own?

It removes the current animals, but without sealing the entry points, new rodents typically follow the same route in. We recommend pairing trapping with exclusion.

How do you handle a rat burrow near my creek-side fence?

We place stations near the burrow entrance and along the fence line, out of reach of pets, and monitor for renewed digging between visits.
