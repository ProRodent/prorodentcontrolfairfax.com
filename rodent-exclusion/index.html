Why sealing matters more than another trap.

A trap removes the rodent that is there right now. It does not close the gap that let it in, which means the next mouse or rat that finds the same scent trail gets the same easy way in. Exclusion is the step that actually ends the cycle, by closing every entry point we find during inspection.

For Fairfax homes, that often means a combination of older-house gaps, such as settled sills and chimney flashing near the Historic District, and newer-construction gaps, such as unsealed utility penetrations closer to the city's outer edges.

Materials that actually hold up.

Steel wool and foam are common do-it-yourself fixes, and both fail over time. Steel wool rusts and loses its grip, and foam gets chewed through within a season, especially once a Virginia winter's freeze-thaw cycle works on it. We use hardware cloth, sheet metal, copper mesh and mortar, matched to the size and location of the gap.

Common Fairfax entry points.

Older homes near Old Town and the Historic District tend to show gaps at chimney flashing, sill plates where the house meets the foundation, and around older window frames that have settled over decades. Newer construction closer to the city's edges more often shows gaps at dryer vents, cable and utility penetrations, and garage door thresholds that were never adjusted correctly.

Crawl space vents are a common weak point across both housing types, since a torn screen or a poorly fitted access door gives rodents a direct route to the space under the living area.

Sealing order matters.

Sealing a gap while rodents are still active inside can trap them in the wall or crawl space, where they die and the smell lingers. That is why exclusion follows treatment: rodent trapping and removal or targeted work through our mouse control or rat control services comes first, and sealing follows once the active population is under control.

  • We inspect the full exterior and interior to mark every gap of 1/4 inch or more.
  • We prioritize active entry points, then close secondary gaps that could be used later.
  • We choose materials by gap size and location, never a one-size-fits-all foam fix.
  • We confirm no animals remain trapped behind a sealed area before finishing the job.

Multiple structures on one property.

Sheds, detached garages and workshops each need their own check, since a gap in an outbuilding gives rodents shelter close to the main house even if the house itself is fully sealed. We treat every structure on the property as part of the same exclusion job, not as an afterthought.

Sealing before a Fairfax winter.

Rodents push toward shelter earlier here than in a milder climate, and sealing before the cold really sets in gives the work the best chance of holding through the season. If your home has had rodent activity in past winters, sealing ahead of this one is worth scheduling early.

Before an exclusion visit.

  • Note any spots where you have seen daylight, drafts or gaps around utility lines.
  • Do not attempt your own foam or steel wool fix before we assess the site.
  • Mention any recent additions, renovations or new utility installations.
  • Clear access to the crawl space, attic hatch and any outbuildings.

After sealing is complete.

Once entry points are closed, we follow up to confirm no new activity appears and that sealed areas are holding. If chewed insulation, ducts or drywall were part of the original damage, our rodent damage repair service addresses that once the entry points are secure.

Additions and renovated sections.

A home with a newer addition attached to an older core often has a seam where the two sections of construction meet, and that seam can develop its own gap separate from anything found on the original structure. We check this transition point specifically on homes with a visible addition, since it is easy to overlook if an inspection only considers the original house and the newer section as two separate, unconnected jobs rather than one continuous exterior.

How long sealing typically holds.

Properly matched materials, sized to the gap and installed correctly, generally hold for years rather than needing yearly reapplication, unlike a foam patch that can fail within a single season. That said, normal wear such as a torn vent screen or a settling foundation can eventually open a new gap even on a well-sealed home. That is why we recommend a periodic check rather than treating exclusion as a permanent, one-time fix that never needs revisiting.

Questions about rodent exclusion and entry point sealing

Why not just use spray foam myself?

Foam looks solid at first but rodents chew through it within a season, and it does not hold up well through repeated freeze-thaw cycles. We use materials that resist chewing and weather.

Will sealing trap an animal inside my wall?

That is why we treat active rodents first, through trapping or a targeted service, before sealing the entry points they were using.

How long does exclusion take?

It depends on how many gaps the inspection finds. A typical home with several entry points can usually be sealed in one visit, with a follow-up to confirm the work is holding.

Do you seal detached garages and sheds too?

Yes. Every structure on the property gets checked and sealed as part of the same job, since a gap in an outbuilding still gives rodents shelter nearby.

Will sealing stop all future rodent problems?

It removes the specific entry points we find, which stops most repeat activity. New wear over time, such as a torn vent screen, can open a fresh gap eventually, which is why we recommend a periodic check.

Is sealing available as a standalone service?

Yes, if an inspection shows no active animals but visible gaps, we can seal without a treatment step first.
