Norway rats and roof rats both show up around Fairfax properties, and the fix depends on which one and where it is traveling.

Norway rats along creeks and foundations.

Norway rats are the larger, ground-dwelling rat most common around Fairfax. They burrow along foundations, under sheds and decks, and beside fence lines, and they follow water and food sources the way a trail leads to a destination. Accotink Creek and Daniels Run, along with the storm drainage that feeds them, give Norway rats a route through the city that connects yard to yard.

A burrow entrance looks like a smooth, worn hole about the size of a tennis ball, often with a shallow, packed-down path leading to it. Homes backing onto a creek, a wooded buffer or a drainage ditch see this pattern most, but any yard with a compost pile, bird feeder or loose trash can draw the same activity.

Roof rats in attics and trees.

Roof rats climb, and they favor attics, upper walls and mature trees with branches that reach toward a roofline. They are less common than Norway rats around Fairfax, but mature tree cover near the Historic District and older streets gives them a route that a Norway rat would not use. Scratching that seems to come from the attic rather than the crawl space is one clue we ask about on the call.

Both rats need the same basic response: find where they travel and where they nest, remove the active animals, and close the entry points so new ones cannot follow the same path.

A burrow does not go away because the rat that dug it moves on.

Why rat colonies grow fast.

A female rat can have 4 to 6 litters a year, with 6 to 12 pups each time, and young rats can breed within a few months. A single burrow you notice in October can support a growing colony by the time spring arrives, especially if a nearby food source, such as an unsecured trash can or bird feeder, keeps supplying them through the cold months.

Because of that pace, removing a few rats without addressing the food source or sealing entry points usually means the population rebounds within weeks. Our approach targets both sides of the problem at once.

Treatment, sealing and yard changes together.

Rat control that lasts combines three things: removing active rats, sealing structural entry points, and reducing the food and shelter that drew them in the first place.

  • We inspect burrows, fence lines, sheds and the foundation, along with attic and roofline access if roof rats are suspected.
  • We set traps and, where appropriate, tamper-resistant bait stations along established travel paths rather than at random spots in the yard.
  • We seal foundation gaps, vent openings and roofline access points with materials rats cannot gnaw through.
  • We point out food sources, such as pet food left outside or an unsecured compost pile, that keep drawing new rats back.

Sealing multiple entry points across a property is covered in detail on our rodent exclusion page, and outdoor burrow treatment is part of our backyard and outdoor rodent control service.

Fence lines, sheds and outbuildings.

Older Fairfax properties near the Historic District often have a detached shed, garage or workshop separate from the main house, and each one is a potential rat shelter on its own. Newer homes closer to the city's edges can still have gaps under a deck or shed foundation that a rat finds within its first season nearby.

We check every structure on the property, not just the main house, since a rat burrow under a shed can support activity that eventually reaches the house itself.

Cold-season rat activity.

Rats push toward shelter as the weather turns cold, the same as mice do, but rats are more likely to stay outdoors in a burrow or crawl space rather than move fully indoors. A burrow near a foundation that seemed dormant in summer can become active again once temperatures drop and the rat looks for a warmer route inside.

Do this before we arrive.

  • Secure trash cans with tight lids and bring pet food indoors overnight.
  • Do not disturb a burrow entrance before the visit.
  • Do not set out loose poison, since it can affect pets and wildlife.
  • Note where you have seen burrow holes, gnawed decking or droppings.
  • Clear access to sheds, crawl spaces and fence lines so we can inspect fully.

What a rat visit involves.

A rat visit typically covers more ground than a mouse call, since burrows and travel paths can span the whole yard. Our rodent inspection and estimate service explains how we scope that inspection before any treatment begins.

Questions about rat control

How do I tell a rat burrow from a groundhog or vole hole?

Rat burrows are smoother, about tennis-ball sized, and usually have a worn path leading to them along a foundation, shed or fence line. If you are unsure, describe the hole and location on the call and we will confirm during the inspection.

Are roof rats common in Fairfax?

Less common than Norway rats, but they do show up, especially near mature trees close to a roofline. Attic scratching alongside nearby tree cover is the pattern we look for.

Can I fill in a burrow myself?

Filling a burrow without removing the rat or sealing the real entry point usually just pushes the rat to dig a new one nearby. We treat the burrow and the structural gap together.

Is rat activity worse near the creek?

Homes near Accotink Creek, Daniels Run or connected drainage tend to see more rat travel, since rats follow water and food sources along those corridors.

Do you handle rats in sheds and detached garages?

Yes. We inspect every structure on the property, not just the main house, since rats often nest in an outbuilding before moving toward a home.

Is service available for a rat emergency at night?

Yes, the line is open 24/7. If a rat is inside your living space, call right away and we will treat it as urgent.
